Climb is the classic genetic algorithm. Illuminate is MAP-Elites: one parent comes from the archive of body shapes below instead of from the current winners, so the search spreads across the whole map of designs rather than collapsing onto one. Measured over six tracks it fills about 40% more of the map and gives up nothing on best distance.

What counts as a good car. Air time breeds jumpers, distance per kilo breeds flimsy ones. Applies from the next generation.

Rank is the original's and is the gentlest of the three: with twenty cars it picks a top five parent about a third of the time. Tournament is the harshest at well over half. Roulette sits between them until one car runs away with it, and then it collapses fast.

Two point keeps runs of neighbouring corners together. Asexual turns this into mutation only, which is a genuinely different search.

Divides a car's fitness by how many near identical cars it is competing with. At zero the gene pool collapses onto one design within a dozen generations. Turn it up and watch the heatmap stay varied.

What this is

This started life as rednuht's Genetic Cars, a 2011 page that was itself a take on BoxCar2D. The idea was lovely and simple: throw random two wheeled shapes at a hill, keep the ones that get furthest, breed them, and watch a car appear out of nothing. That code sat untouched from 2013, running on a Flash port of Box2D.

BoxCar3D is a rebuild of it. Cars now run in three dimensions on Box3D, which is Erin Catto's newer engine and a direct descendant of the Box2D the original used. The road banks from side to side, so a car can roll off the edge instead of only ever getting stuck. Every car carries a small neural network that actually drives it. And the body itself is far more variable: corners rotate as well as stretch, a car has two to four wheels rather than exactly two, and chassis density is a gene.

That last one is the real difference. In the original a wheel spun at one fixed speed forever, so a car was a shape and nothing else. Here the network reads how the car is sitting and moving, looks at the ground three distances ahead, remembers what it was doing a moment ago, and sets the speed of each wheel from all of that. Its 82 weights are genes like any other, so the search is over bodies and drivers at the same time, and you can watch both change in the Driver panel.

Of those, the body turned out to matter most. Measured over twelve runs, a cleverer driver bought almost nothing, while a more variable body improved the average distance the whole population reached by about 15%. The numbers are in the README.

A car scores by how far it gets plus a bonus for average speed. It dies when it stops earning ground, rolls off the edge, or drops into a gap, and the whole generation ends early once nobody is making progress, so one crawler cannot hold everyone up.

The track is a design rather than a dice roll. Length, hills, ramps, gaps, camber and road width, all in the Track panel, all packed into a code about twenty characters long. Send someone yours and they drive the same course hole for hole, which is something the original could not do: it had one kind of terrain, so you could never ask whether a car built for hills could also clear a jump. Medals are fractions of the course, so gold means the same thing on a savage track and a gentle one, and the author medal means a car actually finished.

Today's track is the same one for everybody. It is derived from the date, so two browsers agree on it with nothing in between them, and since a room is keyed to a track code the daily is also the matchmaking: open the page, press join, and you are in with whoever else is driving it. It resets at midnight UTC.

The Map of designs panel is a MAP-Elites archive, out of the quality diversity literature. Every square is a kind of body, and each keeps the best car of that shape ever seen this session, so designs the leaderboard forgets stay findable and clickable. Switch Search to Illuminate and evolution starts breeding across the map instead of only from the winners: measured over six tracks that fills about 40% more of the space and costs nothing on best distance.

Turn Crates up and the road gets cargo on it. They are not scenery: each one is a real body with mass, so it tips, slides down the camber, stacks with its neighbour, and gets shouldered aside by a car with enough momentum while stopping a lighter one dead. That is the same solver the cars run, given something to solve, and it changes what evolution is looking for: a hill is a fixed obstacle a population memorises, a crate moves when you hit it.

The Room panel is the odd one. A genetic algorithm in one tab is alone: it climbs one hill, settles into one local optimum, and waiting is the only way out, while somebody else's tab is stuck somewhere entirely different holding the answer you needed. So everyone on the same track code lands in the same room, and at the end of each generation each tab offers its best car and takes one back. That is the island model, with browsers as the islands. Turn on Colour by family and you can watch a line arrive from someone else's machine and either take over or die out. It is peer to peer with no server, nothing is stored, and it only happens if you press join.

Cars are not the only thing that crosses. The best run on the track is recorded and plays back as a pale ghost racing alongside the pack, and in a room that ghost is whoever is actually fastest, not whoever is nearest. When someone beats the track their lap arrives and starts running on your screen, which is the part borrowed from Trackmania: you are not reading that a number went up, you are watching a line you have to beat. Race the ghost turns it on and off.

The flat mode is still here under 2D. It is the original problem, rebuilt on planck.js, and it is a good place to watch the search when the 3D camera is too busy. Each mode keeps its own records, since they are different problems on different scales.

Physics by Box3D via box3d-wasm, and planck.js in the flat mode. Rendering by three.js. Credit for the original goes to rednuht.
